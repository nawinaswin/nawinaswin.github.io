---
title: "The Hard Problem May Be a Question Asked From the Wrong Side of the Epistemic Boundary"
date: 2023-08-31
---

The thought process develops as an attempt to attack the hard problem of consciousness by temporarily accepting its opposite assumptions and examining what follows.

The starting move is to assume a deterministic universe. An embodied agent exists with memory, including memory traces having an approximately exponentially decaying character. The agent registers events, including events generated by its own actions. It possesses causal models, logic, reason, and some capacity to understand cause and effect. Determinism therefore does not prevent the system from observing its own behavior or constructing representations of the causal consequences of that behavior.

The formulation “without our control” is then deliberately revised. The relevant description is instead an event-driven, reactionary agent: environmental and internal events produce subsequent states and actions. There is no metaphysically free intervention by an independent agent outside the causal chain. Nevertheless, the agent can observe what it does and construct models of what caused what.

The next assumption is stronger: suppose there are no qualitative differences between experiences. No event is intrinsically pleasurable, painful, desirable, aversive, meaningful, or otherwise phenomenally differentiated. The cognitive and causal machinery remains, but qualitative valence is removed.

This produces the central puzzle. A deterministic system can still understand causality, and its representations of causality can themselves causally modify its future internal states. Belief systems therefore become self-referential components of the causal chain. Learning that X causes Y changes the internal distribution from which subsequent behavior emerges. Rearranging beliefs can consequently alter future behavioral trajectories even without metaphysical deliberation or free will.

The distinction is therefore made between agentic deliberation and deterministically produced behavioral distributions. A belief does not have to be freely chosen to causally influence future behavior. Reasoning can operate as part of the deterministic mechanism itself.

But if qualitative differences are completely eliminated, something seemingly important disappears: there is no obvious basis for preferring one possible trajectory over another. Reason and logic could still establish what causes what, but there would be no intrinsic qualitative distinction establishing that one consequence is preferable, undesirable, significant, or worth pursuing. Everything could become indifferentiably null and void.

This motivates the hypothesis that qualitative differentiation may have a functional connection to motivation, valuation, and the organization of future behavior, rather than merely being an inexplicable decorative accompaniment to computation.

A neurological aside then enters the reasoning: there is a vague recollection of neurological cases in which affective components of emotion can be disrupted or dissociated while relevant signaling and cognitive processing remain present. Such cases might empirically test whether emotional/qualitative components are separable from the underlying informational and causal machinery. Specific patient names are not recalled, but the general phenomenon is recognized as an established area of neuroscience.

The discussion then turns directly toward the formulation of the hard problem. Two possible interpretations are distinguished:

1. Why does subjective experience exist at all?
2. Why do different experiences possess different qualitative characters?

The first formulation is regarded as potentially problematic because, under sufficiently strict epistemological standards, only one’s own apparent experience is directly available. The existence of other subjects’ experiences is abductively inferred from behavioral, biological, structural, and contextual similarity. Even the assertion that subjective experience exists is known through the system’s own self-representation rather than from an external, absolute standpoint.

The objection then becomes metaphysical. Asking why subjective experience exists at all may resemble asking why existence, information, or particular forms of organization exist at all. If physical systems can contain information that stands in relations to other information, there is no immediately evident reason why information could not possess relational or structural properties that constitute what is described as qualitative character.

A conceptual model follows: the mind can be understood as a highly fuzzy knowledge graph. It does not contain perfectly discrete objects connected by perfectly discrete edges. Instead, it contains overlapping, dynamically weighted structures in which representations are fuzzily associated with memories, predictions, concepts, bodily states, other representations, and contextual information.

Crucially, each node or representational structure can possess not merely informational relations but a qualitative attribute. The qualitative character is therefore conceptualized as something associated with the structure of the information-processing system itself rather than as an additional metaphysical substance mysteriously attached to computation.

Physical processes can then, at least abductively, explain why particular qualitative characters correspond to particular events, objects, states, or representations. There can be mechanistic accounts of which physical processes produce particular qualitative configurations.

However, such explanations may never satisfy someone demanding a fundamentally deeper answer. The demand for an explanation of why a particular physical/informational organization is a particular qualitative experience may itself move beyond empirical explanation into metaphysics.

This produces the strongest critique of the hard problem: perhaps the “explanatory gap” results from demanding an answer of a category that empirical explanation cannot provide. A complete causal and structural account may explain the phenomenon, while the residual question—why that organization constitutes that experience rather than something else—may amount to asking for an ultimate metaphysical identification rather than another mechanism.

That is where the Kantian connection becomes apparent. The framework does not appear particularly novel or revolutionary when viewed from the standpoint of Kantian epistemology. The relevant distinction between what can be known from within cognition, how cognition structures experience, and what can legitimately be demanded as knowledge has antecedents roughly two centuries earlier.

The surprisingly large citation counts of contemporary consciousness papers therefore appear disproportionate to the perceived philosophical novelty. The contemporary literature may have reformulated older epistemological and metaphysical problems in the terminology of analytic philosophy, cognitive science, and neuroscience, while presenting the resulting question as a distinctive contemporary problem.

The overall position consequently develops into something more specific than “consciousness comes from information.” It is a challenge to the legitimacy of the demand for an explanation beyond the physical/informational structure itself. Once the causal organization, representational structure, relational dynamics, memory, valuation, and qualitative differentiation of the system are explained, demanding a further explanation of why that structure constitutes experience may not necessarily identify an unresolved scientific mechanism. It may instead constitute a metaphysical demand for an answer from outside the very cognitive framework through which the question is generated.
