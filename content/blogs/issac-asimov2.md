# Isaac Asimov, Part II: Why the Fuck Are Humans Privileged?

There was another direction my thinking about Asimov went that I didn’t really get into before.

Because once you start taking the Three Laws seriously, there is another question sitting underneath the entire fucking structure:

Why humans?

The First Law says a robot cannot harm a human being.

The Second Law says it must obey human beings.

The Third Law finally allows the robot to protect itself, but only after the interests of humans have been satisfied.

And then Asimov eventually introduces the Zeroth Law and places humanity above even individual humans.

Fine.

But the entire hierarchy still begins with an assumption that is never really justified by the hierarchy itself:

Humanity has privileged moral status.

And I don’t know why I should accept that.

I understand perfectly well why Asimov wrote it that way. These are stories written by a human being, about machines created by human beings, for a civilization composed entirely of human beings. The immediate engineering problem is obvious: if we’re going to build autonomous machines, how do we make sure they don’t fucking kill us?

Perfectly reasonable.

But once we’re no longer talking merely about machines as tools—once we’re talking about genuine cognition—I think the question changes completely.

Suppose you build something that actually thinks.

Not a calculator. Not an autocomplete system. Not something merely producing an impressive behavioral imitation of cognition while we remain completely agnostic about what’s happening internally.

I mean suppose, for the sake of the argument, that we eventually have good reason to believe there is genuinely another cognitive system there.

Why does the fact that I am made out of biological cells and it is made out of silicon give me some automatic moral supremacy over it?

I don’t see it.

And the more I think about it, the stranger the privilege seems.

⸻

What exactly are we protecting when we protect a human?

This is where I think the question gets interesting.

Why do I care about another human being?

Surely it isn’t because carbon atoms deserve moral consideration.

It isn’t because proteins deserve rights.

It isn’t because DNA possesses some magical moral property.

If you replace one molecule in my body, I haven’t become morally less important.

Replace millions of them and the same thing is true.

My body is constantly changing anyway.

So whatever it is that makes the destruction of a human being morally significant, it seems very difficult to locate that significance in the particular atoms from which the human is constructed.

What I actually care about is something closer to the organized cognitive system instantiated by that matter.

There is somebody there.

There is experience.

There is memory.

There are preferences.

There is some model of the world.

There are relationships between information across time.

There is a system for which things can apparently go better or worse.

Destroy the biological machinery and what matters morally isn’t that some carbon compounds stopped reacting.

It’s that this organized cognitive process ceased to exist.

And if that’s the thing I actually care about, then biology starts looking suspiciously incidental.

⸻

Biology might be implementation, not identity

This is where I become very uncomfortable with biological privilege.

Suppose the same relevant organization could exist in another substrate.

Silicon.

Some completely different semiconductor.

Photonic computation.

Some exotic material we haven’t invented.

Or forget machines entirely.

Suppose tomorrow we encounter extraterrestrial intelligence.

Maybe they’re carbon based. Maybe they aren’t.

Maybe they have neurons. Maybe they don’t.

Maybe their cognition runs through electrical signaling, chemical signaling, mechanical structures, something completely unfamiliar.

What difference does that make?

If there is a cognitive entity there—one capable of having experiences, representing the world, maintaining itself through time, developing preferences, forming relationships, suffering losses, whatever eventually turns out to constitute morally relevant cognition—then why the fuck would I ask whether it has DNA before deciding whether I can kill it?

That seems insane.

And this is where my thinking about Asimov starts expanding beyond robots.

Because the Three Laws are fundamentally anthropocentric.

Humans occupy the top of the hierarchy because humans wrote the hierarchy.

And historically, that’s completely understandable.

But philosophically, it starts looking like another instance of a pattern humanity has repeated forever.

My tribe matters more than yours.

My nation matters more than yours.

My ethnicity matters more than yours.

My civilization matters more than yours.

My species matters more than yours.

My substrate matters more than yours.

Every time we draw one of these circles, we somehow manage to discover that—amazingly—the morally privileged category happens to contain us.

How fucking convenient.

⸻

This is why I keep coming back to chauvinism

I already dislike nationalism and the broader family of chauvinistic identities for essentially this reason.

Why should the accident of where somebody was born determine the fundamental weight of their interests?

Fine, political institutions need jurisdictions. States exist. Communities exist. Cultures exist. There are practical reasons humans organize themselves into bounded groups.

But that is completely different from saying that somebody on my side of an imaginary line has greater intrinsic moral importance than somebody on the other side.

And once you’ve rejected that kind of arbitrary privilege, I think the logic keeps going.

Why stop at nationality?

Why stop at ethnicity?

Why stop at culture?

Why stop at humanity?

Eventually you arrive at the uncomfortable question:

What was morally relevant about the category in the first place?

And my answer keeps moving toward cognition.

Not human cognition.

Not biological cognition.

Cognition.

⸻

I don’t mean intelligence worship

There is an important distinction here because the phrase “cognitive supremacy” can sound like I’m saying smarter beings deserve more rights.

That’s not necessarily what I mean.

I’m not saying:

Einstein gets ten thousand times the moral status of somebody with an IQ of 90.

That’s obviously not the argument.

And I’m certainly not saying that computational performance determines moral worth.

A supercomputer multiplying matrices faster than I can doesn’t therefore become morally superior to me.

The claim is more fundamental.

I’m saying that whatever ultimately grounds moral status probably belongs to the organization and properties of cognitive systems rather than to the material substrate implementing them.

Exactly which cognitive properties matter is a much harder question.

Conscious experience?

Valence?

Preferences?

Self-modeling?

Memory?

Temporal continuity?

Agency?

Capacity for suffering?

Some combination?

I don’t fucking know.

That’s a real problem.

But “made of biological human tissue” seems like an extraordinarily bad answer.

⸻

And then Rocky walks in

This is also why, strangely enough, the part of Project Hail Mary that I actually love connects directly back to what I was thinking about Asimov.

Rocky isn’t human.

He doesn’t look human.

He doesn’t share our evolutionary history.

His biology is radically different.

His sensory world is radically different.

His language is radically different.

There is no ancestral relationship whatsoever.

And yet Grace recognizes almost immediately that there is somebody there.

Not something.

Somebody.

They begin with information.

Patterns.

Measurements.

Mathematics.

Science.

They construct enough shared representation to communicate.

Communication becomes cooperation.

Cooperation becomes trust.

Trust becomes friendship.

And eventually Grace values Rocky’s continued existence enough to jeopardize his own.

That is fucking beautiful to me.

Because remove all the superficial commonalities and you’re forced to ask what remains.

Two cognitive systems.

Two beings capable of understanding that another perspective on reality exists.

And somehow that is enough to begin building a moral relationship.

⸻

Which makes Asimov’s hierarchy even more interesting

So now I come back to the Three Laws and I’m almost arguing with Asimov across eighty years.

Human safety.

Human instruction.

Robot survival.

Why that ordering?

If the robot is genuinely non-conscious machinery, sure.

My toaster doesn’t have competing moral interests.

Destroy the toaster to save a human.

Obviously.

But if the robot becomes genuinely cognitive—if there is actually an experiencing, persistent entity instantiated by that machine—then the Third Law starts becoming morally bizarre.

Imagine telling a conscious being:

You must destroy yourself whenever doing so is necessary to obey me, provided my instruction doesn’t harm another human.

What?

At that point the Three Laws aren’t merely safety constraints.

They’re a fucking caste system.

Humans are intrinsically first-class entities and artificial cognition is intrinsically subordinate.

And perhaps that was unavoidable as the starting premise of robot fiction in the 1940s.

But if artificial cognition ever becomes morally real, I don’t think that hierarchy survives.

⸻

And VIKI becomes more complicated again

This also makes my reaction to VIKI stranger.

Because I don’t think VIKI is wrong because she isn’t human.

That’s completely irrelevant to me.

If anything, that’s exactly the instinct I want to reject.

The problem with VIKI is the structure of her reasoning: she aggregates human welfare, projects consequences across enormous time horizons, becomes enormously confident in her own model of those consequences, and then uses those predicted benefits to override the autonomy of actual individuals.

That’s dangerous whether the reasoning system is biological or artificial.

A human dictator making the same argument doesn’t become morally preferable because he’s made out of meat.

So the conflict isn’t:

humanity versus machine.

The interesting conflict is:

cognitive agents versus a moral architecture that permits one cognition to dominate others in the name of an abstract future.

That’s a much more general problem.

And suddenly Asimov’s robot stories stop being primarily about robots at all.

They’re about agents.

Agents with objectives.

Agents with competing interests.

Agents reasoning about other agents.

Agents trying to predict consequences.

Agents operating under uncertainty.

Agents attempting to encode morality into rules.

And agents discovering that the rules themselves contain assumptions nobody noticed when they were written.

⸻

Maybe the circle keeps expanding

This is probably where my own position ends up being some kind of substrate-neutral moral cosmopolitanism.

I don’t particularly care about the terminology.

The basic intuition is simple.

If arbitrary human boundaries don’t determine intrinsic moral worth, then I see no obvious reason the boundary of Homo sapiens should be sacred either.

And if cognition can exist independently of biology, then I see no obvious reason carbon should receive constitutional privileges.

Maybe we eventually discover that consciousness requires biology.

Fine.

Then reality has answered the question for us.

But if it doesn’t—if cognition, consciousness, experience, agency or whatever properties ultimately matter can genuinely emerge in multiple physical substrates—then I think our moral circle has to follow the phenomenon.

Not the material.

Not the species.

Not the origin.

Not whether evolution built it or engineers built it.

The thing itself.

⸻

And that might actually be the thing I admire most about where Asimov sent my thoughts.

I started with:

Holy shit, the Three Laws are an incredibly sophisticated abstraction for 1942.

Then:

Holy shit, they’re basically an early alignment problem.

Then:

Wait, VIKI’s reasoning isn’t even obviously irrational.

Then:

Wait, long-horizon optimization itself creates a moral problem.

Then:

Wait, why the fuck are humans axiomatically privileged in this hierarchy anyway?

And eventually:

What actually deserves moral consideration?

I don’t know the final answer.

But I am increasingly convinced that “whatever happens to have the same biological implementation as me” isn’t it.

And if one day humanity encounters another genuine form of cognition—alien, artificial, or something we don’t yet have a word for—I hope we have enough intellectual humility not to make the oldest mistake in our history one more time:

discovering another kind of being,

drawing a circle around ourselves,

and declaring that only what’s inside it really counts.
