Alexandria, Hoboken: The Doppelgängers

It’s interesting that, you know, I’ve lived in Hoboken when I went to Stevens Institute of Technology, and I’ve also spent a good amount of time in DC and Virginia, including Alexandria. And come to think of it, Alexandria and Hoboken have a lot of similarities, don’t they?

Obviously, looking at the map from up, the grid-like structure of Old Town Alexandria and Hoboken looks very similar. And that’s one of the basic similarities. Both have this sort of square, roughly one-square-mile urban structure along a river waterfront, which is kind of odd when you think about it. Alexandria itself is much larger, of course, but the historic grid of Old Town is almost comparable in size to Hoboken. Hoboken might be a little smaller, but they’re in the same ballpark.

And then there’s the waterfront. Both have this very distinct relationship with the river, with the grid extending toward it, and with the transportation infrastructure sitting along the edges of the urban structure.

In Hoboken, you have the Hoboken Terminal, the PATH, New Jersey Transit trains, the Hudson-Bergen Light Rail, and the buses, all concentrated around that edge of the city. And historically, on the other side, you had Weehawken, which was also a major rail and ferry terminal before the development of the underwater rail connections to Manhattan. So you had these compact urban areas, with transportation infrastructure concentrated along their edges, connecting them to a much larger metropolitan area.

And Alexandria has something similar. You have the historic grid of Old Town, the waterfront, and then the transportation infrastructure around the edges, including the rail lines, Metro, and highways connecting the area to the wider Washington metropolitan region. It’s not exactly the same arrangement, obviously, but there’s something similar about how these urban spaces relate to the transportation networks around them.

And it’s not just the physical structure, either. Both Hoboken and Old Town Alexandria have a pretty strong sense of community, or at least that’s the impression I get from how people talk about them. They have this distinct identity, despite being part of much larger metropolitan areas.

I don’t know. There’s nothing particularly analytical about this observation, and I’m not necessarily trying to make some larger argument about urban planning or the historical development of cities. It’s just one of those things you notice when you’ve spent time in two different places and, at some point, realize that they’re oddly similar.

Almost as if they’re doppelgängers, in a way. Different histories, different rivers, different cities, but a surprisingly similar structure.
