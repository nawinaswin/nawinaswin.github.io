Blade Runner 2049: The Absurdity of Wanting to Be Special

I recently rewatched Blade Runner 2049, and I think it’s an extraordinary film. What fascinates me is how it brings together science fiction, existentialism, and absurdism in a way that very few movies manage to accomplish.

Of course, science fiction has always been interested in questions of existence. Our curiosity about the universe, our place in it, and what makes us human is practically built into the genre. But Blade Runner 2049 does something more interesting. It takes these existential questions and pushes them toward something absurdist.

Consider K, the protagonist. He’s searching for identity, belonging, and, in some sense, love. He wants to believe that he is special. He wants his existence to mean something beyond the mechanical circumstances of his creation. And the film encourages us to believe it, too.

For a while, it seems that K might be extraordinary. Perhaps he’s the exception, the one who was born rather than manufactured. Perhaps his suffering and alienation have some greater significance. Perhaps there’s something fundamentally different about him that makes his existence matter.

And then the film pulls the rug out from under his feet.

He’s not the special one. He’s not the exception. He’s just another replicant.

I find this reversal genuinely funny in an almost absurdist sense. The film spends so much time constructing the possibility that K is exceptional, only to dismantle it. All that longing, all that anticipation, all that emotional investment in the possibility of being somebody special—and then, essentially, nothing.

But there’s something else happening here, too.

We tend to believe that there’s some relationship between virtue and outcomes. We want to believe that good people are rewarded, that suffering has a purpose, that extraordinary sacrifices are somehow vindicated. There’s an almost karmic expectation that events should flow in a particular direction, that goodness and significance should have some causal relationship.

And perhaps this is precisely what makes K’s story so interesting.

We initially interpret his possible origins as evidence of his significance. We want his suffering to mean something. We want his existence to be justified by some extraordinary quality. Even when the film reveals that he isn’t the child he’s been looking for, it still portrays him as virtuous through his final actions.

And there’s a strange contradiction here. The film dismantles the idea that K is cosmically special, but it still gives him a morally satisfying conclusion. We lose the grand revelation of his identity, but we retain the heroic arc.

It’s almost as if we cannot entirely escape the need to believe that goodness must amount to something.

But what if it doesn’t?

What if there’s no underlying causal relationship between virtue and reward? What if the universe doesn’t care whether you’re good, whether you suffer, or whether your sacrifices lead to anything meaningful? What if things simply happen, without any overarching moral logic connecting them?

Life is indifferent. It doesn’t owe us a coherent narrative. It doesn’t owe us justice, recognition, or even an explanation.

And perhaps that’s the absurdity of it all.

We want to be special. We want to belong. We want our lives to mean something. We want to believe that our virtues distinguish us, that our suffering is justified, and that our choices contribute to something larger than ourselves.

But the universe doesn’t promise any of these things.

So what do we do with that realization?

I’m not entirely sure that we need to arrive at some profound conclusion. Perhaps we don’t need to replace one grand narrative with another. Perhaps we don’t need to turn the absence of cosmic meaning into a new philosophy of heroic self-creation.

Maybe there’s something almost comical about the whole enterprise.

We spend our lives searching for meaning, constructing identities, and trying to establish that we’re somehow exceptional. We invent stories about why things happen and what they mean. Then we discover that the universe isn’t obligated to cooperate with any of those stories.

And then what?

Fuck it. There’s no grand cosmic accounting system. No guarantee that virtue will be rewarded or that suffering will be redeemed. No promise that being special will make your existence more meaningful.

Do whatever the fuck you want. Find something interesting. Care about something, if you can. Enjoy yourself. And move the fuck on.

Not because this is necessarily the correct philosophy of life, but because perhaps we don’t need to turn the absence of a universal purpose into another existential problem that must be solved.

That’s what I find so compelling about Blade Runner 2049. Its bleakness isn’t merely a matter of its dystopian setting, muted colors, or the desolation of its world. The entire film seems to draw us into a particular emotional and philosophical atmosphere, one in which our desire for identity, belonging, and significance gradually collides with the indifference of existence.

And even after dismantling the possibility that K is special, the film still wants us to find his final actions meaningful.

Perhaps that’s a contradiction. Perhaps it’s simply the nature of storytelling. Or perhaps the film is suggesting that something can matter without being cosmically ordained to matter.

I don’t know.

But I find the tension fascinating. We want to believe that we’re special, and we construct narratives to justify that belief. Then, when those narratives collapse, we struggle to accept that nothing necessarily replaces them.

Maybe the real absurdity isn’t that life has no predetermined meaning. It’s that we keep expecting it to provide one.

And perhaps we can recognize that absurdity, laugh at it, and carry on anyway.
