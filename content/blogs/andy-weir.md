# My Andy Weir Conundrum: I Buy The Martian. I Don’t Buy Project Hail Mary

I think I finally figured out what bothers me so fucking much about Project Hail Mary, especially compared with The Martian.

It isn’t simply that Project Hail Mary is scientifically unrealistic. Plenty of science fiction is scientifically unrealistic. I don’t have some rule that fiction must confine itself to things we could publish in Nature tomorrow. You can have wormholes. You can have aliens. You can speculate about black holes, dark matter, strange chemistry, unknown phases of matter, whatever. That’s what fiction is for.

What bothers me is that if you’re going to present yourself as scientifically rigorous, then respect the fucking boundary between what we don’t know and what we already know.

Those are not the same thing.

We have enormous holes in our understanding of the universe. We don’t know what dark matter is. We don’t have quantum gravity. We can ask extremely fundamental questions about entropy, initial conditions, why the constants have the values they have, why there is something rather than nothing, whatever. Fine.

But the existence of those holes does not mean that everything between them is suddenly negotiable.

Physics has domains in which it is unbelievably well tested. Chemistry has constraints. Thermodynamics has constraints. Materials have constraints. Molecular structures have temperature ranges in which they can exist. You can’t point toward the fact that fundamental physics remains incomplete and use that as a permission slip to insert essentially arbitrary higher-order phenomena into regimes that we understand rather well.

And Astrophage feels exactly like that to me.

This thing is biological. It reproduces. It interacts with ordinary chemistry. It apparently survives stellar environments. It stores absolutely ridiculous quantities of energy. It releases that energy in a useful way. It provides the means for interstellar propulsion. It causes the central catastrophe. It can be experimentally manipulated. And then, conveniently, there is another microscopic organism that fucking eats it.

At some point, what are we doing here?

And what makes it worse for me is that the movie then goes right back to being scientifically serious.

Now we’re measuring things. Now we’re doing experiments. Now we’re calculating trajectories. Now we’re reasoning from observations. Now we’re doing engineering. Look how clever the science is.

Well, hang on.

You just introduced a microorganism that can hang around a fucking star and behave like an absurdly energy-dense battery.

You don’t get to demand that I admire the precision of the calculations five minutes later without my brain going back to the gigantic magical object on which the calculations depend.

That’s the difference for me between Project Hail Mary and The Martian.

The Martian absolutely cheats. The opening dust storm is the obvious example. Mars has dust storms, including enormous ones, but the atmosphere is so thin that the aerodynamic forces are nowhere near what the movie requires. Fine. That’s wrong.

But look at the kind of wrong it is.

The atmosphere exists. The storm exists. The spacecraft exists. The forces involved exist. The movie essentially turns a parameter knob too far because it needs a mechanism to strand Watney.

That irritates me far less because the rest of the universe hasn’t changed.

You could rewrite the opening with a more plausible accident—a structural failure, loss of telemetry, terrain collapse, a launch emergency, whatever—and almost the entire rest of The Martian survives untouched.

Astrophage isn’t like that. Remove Astrophage and Project Hail Mary loses its catastrophe, its energy source, its propulsion mechanism, its central scientific mystery and eventually its solution. The miracle isn’t incidental. The entire machine is built around it.

And the frustrating thing is: I actually really like the central moral idea of Project Hail Mary.

Maybe even love it.

Strip away Astrophage and there is a beautiful idea underneath this story.

Two intelligent beings emerge from completely independent evolutionary histories. They share no species, no planet, no biology, no culture, no language, no ancestry. They meet in an incomprehensibly enormous universe in which each civilization has apparently spent its entire history believing itself alone.

And somehow they recognize one another.

Science becomes their first bridge. Observation. Pattern. Mathematics. Experiment. Eventually language. Then trust. Then friendship. And finally moral concern.

Grace’s arc makes that stronger. He isn’t originally the heroic volunteer we assume he is. He was afraid. He didn’t want to die. He was forced into the mission. But eventually he gets another choice, and this time he voluntarily risks giving up his own return because another intelligent being—and another civilization entirely—will die otherwise.

That’s a fucking good theme.

It says something genuinely interesting: that moral concern might survive the removal of every superficial similarity between two beings. That reason, curiosity, cooperation and perhaps compassion might be deeper commonalities than species or substrate.

So why the hell did we need Astrophage?

You could construct this story in fifty different ways while respecting scientific uncertainty.

Humanity develops the ability to visit some genuinely extraordinary astrophysical object. Maybe there’s a nearby black hole we can finally investigate directly. Of course we’d fucking go. We wouldn’t even need an extinction event. Knowledge itself is enough motivation for civilization to undertake something enormous.

Something goes wrong. Most of the crew dies. Grace survives partly through circumstance. Another civilization independently sends an expedition to investigate the same object. Rocky is there.

There. We have our story.

Or, if you absolutely need the existential threat, use stellar activity.

Suppose the Sun enters some unprecedented regime of extreme solar activity. Humanity discovers an exoplanet orbiting an even more violent star which, according to everything we understand, should have had its atmosphere stripped away or its surface sterilized ages ago.

Except it hasn’t.

Now that’s interesting.

Maybe the planet has an extraordinary magnetic field. Maybe its interior contains some unusual composition producing an enormous dynamo. Maybe there’s some combination of atmospheric conductivity, induced magnetic fields and planetary structure that we’ve never encountered before. Maybe our existing theories remain correct but this particular configuration produces an emergent shielding mechanism nobody anticipated.

Now humanity has a reason to go there.

And perhaps Rocky’s civilization has exactly the same problem.

Both species independently discover the same anomaly. Both travel there because they need to understand it. Both arrive expecting only a natural phenomenon and instead discover each other.

You still get the existential pressure. You still get the scientific mystery. You still get the isolation. You still get Grace and Rocky. You still get science as their common language. You still get cooperation. You still get sacrifice. You still get the entire moral fucking point.

Except now the mystery occupies territory where we can honestly say: we don’t know.

That’s what I mean by respecting the limits of knowledge.

Interstellar is useful as a comparison here. That movie takes gigantic liberties too. Wormholes are speculative. The tesseract is wildly speculative. Communication across time through gravity is speculative as hell.

But where does the movie take us?

A black hole.

That is precisely the sort of place where our current theories genuinely become incomplete. General relativity itself leads us toward regimes where we know we need physics we don’t yet possess. If you want to tell me that characters venture into some extreme gravitational environment and encounter something our current science cannot completely describe, I can grant you enormous latitude there.

You are taking your speculation into the darkness on the map.

My problem with Astrophage is that it feels like somebody drew a dragon in the middle of fucking France and then said, “Well, technically, cartography isn’t complete.”

And that’s ultimately my problem with the defense that science fiction gets “one miracle.”

Sure. It can.

But there are different kinds of miracles.

You can introduce a speculative phenomenon in a genuinely unresolved region of physics and ask what follows from it. Or you can simply invent something with whatever collection of properties the plot requires and call that your one assumption.

Astrophage doesn’t even feel like one assumption to me. It’s a bundle of assumptions disguised as one noun.

Stellar survival. Extraordinary energy storage. Energy conversion. Propulsion. Reproduction. Ordinary-matter interaction. Directed behavior. Interplanetary migration. All packaged into one organism.

That’s not one miracle. That’s six miracles wearing a trench coat.

And apparently Andy Weir is perfectly capable of doing the other thing. The Martian demonstrates it. He can take a constrained physical environment and extract drama from the constraints themselves.

That’s what makes this more frustrating.

I am not a physicist. I’m not claiming mastery of astrophysics, chemistry, biology or anything else. And sitting around thinking about this for a few minutes, I can already imagine multiple premises that preserve almost everything interesting about Project Hail Mary without requiring its central scientific monstrosity.

So surely Andy Weir could too.

Which makes me think this wasn’t inability. It was a choice.

And I can understand the choice. Astrophage is an extraordinarily efficient plot device. It creates the crisis, enables the mission, powers the spacecraft, supplies the scientific puzzles and eventually leads to the solution. From a storytelling perspective, that’s economical as hell.

But economy isn’t the same thing as elegance.

If you want to write science fantasy, write science fantasy. I have no objection to that.

If you want one enormous impossible premise and then an internally consistent universe built around it, fine. Tell me that’s the game we’re playing.

And if you want hard science fiction, then respect what makes hard science fiction interesting in the first place: the universe does not rearrange itself for the protagonist. The constraints are the fucking story.

What I can’t stand is jumping between those territories whenever convenient.

Don’t ask me to suspend physical reality for your central plot mechanism and then immediately turn around and ask me to appreciate how carefully somebody calculated the trajectory.

Pick a lane.

Because the tragedy is that underneath all of this, Project Hail Mary has a moral idea I genuinely love: two utterly alien forms of cognition meeting in the enormous loneliness of the universe and discovering that intelligence, curiosity, cooperation and moral concern can cross a boundary that evolution never prepared either of them to cross.

That idea doesn’t need magic.

If anything, it deserves better than magic.
