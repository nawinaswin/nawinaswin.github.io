Dutch Date

I’ve been thinking about who pays on a first date. Not because I have a particularly strong opinion about whether men should pay, but because I find the reasoning behind people’s preferences interesting. I’m reasonably self-aware about my own preferences, or at least I try to be, but knowing what I prefer and understanding why I prefer it are two different things.

And that’s where I started thinking about women who identify as feminists but still prefer men to pay for dates.

I don’t want to assume that these women are irrational or hypocritical. Quite the opposite, actually. I think most people have comparable capacities for rational thought, even if they differ in experience, knowledge, and analytical ability. So surely there are women who have thought about this question, who consider themselves feminists, and who can give a perfectly rational account of why they still prefer traditional courtship. I was interested in understanding what that account might look like.

Perhaps a woman genuinely likes a man paying for her. Perhaps she associates it with generosity, affection, or a willingness to invest in a relationship. Perhaps a man genuinely likes paying for someone. I don’t think individual preferences necessarily require any elaborate justification. People enjoy doing things for other people. There’s nothing particularly mysterious about that.

But I started wondering how much of this preference is actually individual and how much of it comes from the expectations surrounding it. If a woman grows up in a culture where men paying for dates is considered normal, where it signals masculinity or romantic interest, how do we distinguish between something she independently prefers and something she’s learned to prefer? And the same question applies to men. If a man grows up believing that paying is what a man is supposed to do, how much of his preference is independent of that expectation?

I’m not suggesting that cultural conditioning invalidates a preference. That would be a strange conclusion, considering that culture influences practically everything about us. But I do think it complicates the argument that a practice is acceptable simply because someone genuinely enjoys it.

After all, if someone has been conditioned to expect something, the fact that they want it doesn’t tell us everything about the conditions that produced that want.

And then there’s the question of whether the choice is actually free. Suppose a man would prefer to split the bill, but he knows that doing so might make him appear cheap or insufficiently interested. He pays because the alternative carries a social cost. Now suppose a woman would be perfectly happy splitting the bill, but she believes that offering to do so would undermine the romantic dynamic or make her appear less feminine. She doesn’t offer.

Neither person necessarily needs to consciously endorse the convention. Neither has to be explicitly coerced by the other. They might both be making perfectly understandable decisions given the expectations they face. Yet their decisions help reproduce the very convention that constrains their options.

This is where I think game theory becomes relevant. Individually rational behavior doesn’t necessarily produce a collectively desirable outcome. A convention can persist because deviating from it is costly, even when the people participating in it might prefer a different arrangement if the expectations around them were different. I’m not claiming that this is necessarily what’s happening in dating, or that most men secretly want to split the bill. I’m saying that the observed behavior alone doesn’t establish what people would prefer under different incentives.

And if that’s the case, then saying that two people consent to an arrangement doesn’t completely resolve the question of whether the arrangement is desirable. Consent matters, obviously, but it doesn’t tell us everything about the social conditions under which people make their choices.

This is partly why I find certain ideological arguments frustrating. I’m a big fan of Chomsky, even though I think he has some pretty extreme left-wing positions that I don’t necessarily agree with. What I find interesting about his broader approach is the emphasis on examining the structures within which individual behavior occurs. I also recall criticism of feminism as sometimes being too narrow a cause, focusing on gender while neglecting wider structural problems. I wouldn’t want to attribute that exact formulation to him without checking the source, but the general concern resonates with me.

My problem isn’t with examining gender inequality. It’s with treating an explanation of one aspect of a system as if it were an explanation of the system itself.

Take the dating example. We could argue that men being expected to pay reflects an unequal gender norm. That might be true. But have we explained why the norm exists? Have we explained the incentives that keep it going? Have we considered the economic conditions, cultural expectations, and individual preferences that interact to produce it? And if we change the convention, what actually happens?

I don’t think answering the first question automatically answers the others. Sometimes I feel that ideological arguments move too quickly from identifying a problem to prescribing a solution, without adequately accounting for the other variables that the solution might affect. This isn’t a problem exclusive to feminism. Any ideology can become so focused on its own explanatory framework that it neglects the larger system in which the problem exists.

Then I remembered something that made the whole question even more interesting. I have a friend who genuinely believes that paying for women is a good way to subjugate them. His reasoning is that spending money gives a man leverage, establishes masculine superiority, and creates an expectation that the woman should behave as a subordinate.

Now, consider a man who pays because he genuinely enjoys being generous, and another who pays because he believes it’s his masculine duty. All three men might perform exactly the same action. But their motivations are completely different. One sees generosity, another sees tradition, and my friend sees an opportunity to establish control.

Superficially, the behavior is identical. The meaning of the behavior isn’t.

And even if we understand the individual’s motivation, we still haven’t necessarily established the social consequences of the practice. A man might sincerely enjoy providing without wanting to control anyone, while the broader convention might still reinforce the expectation that men should bear financial responsibility for courtship. Conversely, a woman can genuinely enjoy being treated without believing that she owes the man anything in return.

So I don’t think we can conclude that paying for a date is inherently oppressive. But I also don’t think we can conclude that it’s socially neutral simply because some people enjoy it. We need to consider motivation, the freedom to opt out, the incentives people face, and the wider consequences of the convention.

And this is where I start wondering what we’re actually trying to optimize. If two people genuinely enjoy a traditional arrangement, why should we insist that they behave differently? But if we say that individual satisfaction is sufficient justification, what do we do with arrangements that satisfy the preferences of the people participating in them while imposing costs elsewhere or sustaining expectations that disadvantage other people?

A utilitarian analysis might ask whether the practice improves overall well-being. A game-theoretic analysis might ask whether it persists as an equilibrium despite being collectively suboptimal. A feminist analysis might ask whether it reproduces unequal expectations. These approaches aren’t necessarily mutually exclusive, but they aren’t asking precisely the same question either.

And I don’t think any one of them, by itself, gives us a complete account.

There’s also a danger in taking the critique of cultural conditioning too far. If every preference is shaped by prior causes, then what exactly are we supposed to do with that observation? Are we going to dismiss everything people enjoy as a product of conditioning? Surely not. The fact that a preference has a causal history doesn’t establish that it’s undesirable, any more than the fact that a preference is sincerely held establishes that it’s desirable.

I find this particularly interesting because I tend to push analytical arguments to their extremes. If you take determinism seriously, you can start questioning the foundations of practically every preference, objective, and moral judgment. Nothing exists independently of its causal history, including the standards by which we evaluate things. You can keep following that line of reasoning until you start undermining the very objectives that make reasoning useful to you.

I’ve experienced something like this myself. If you dismantle every preference and every goal, you can eventually end up in a state where nothing seems worth pursuing. My analytical reasoning hasn’t necessarily failed at that point; the problem is that the analysis has undermined the reward functions through which I evaluate outcomes in the first place.

I think cognition itself requires some persistence of expectations. In reinforcement learning, an agent needs some criterion against which outcomes can be evaluated. Temporal-difference learning, for instance, updates predictions of future returns, and those predictions help guide subsequent behavior. I’m not claiming that this settles the philosophy of human motivation, but it illustrates why a system that continually dismantles every possible objective risks losing the basis on which it chooses between alternatives.

At some point, you have to operate with objectives that matter to you, even while recognizing that those objectives have causes and aren’t necessarily objectively privileged in some absolute sense.

And perhaps something similar applies to social systems. We can question the origins of our preferences, examine the incentives that sustain our conventions, and criticize the consequences of our choices without pretending that we’re going to arrive at a perfectly rational, universally optimal society. We still have to live and interact with the imperfect systems that exist, and figure out how to navigate them.

Which brings me back to the original question. Should a man pay for a first date? I don’t have a universal answer, and I’m not sure there needs to be one. If he wants to pay, he can pay. If they want to split the bill, they can split it. I don’t think equality requires everyone to behave identically, and I don’t think traditional behavior is automatically evidence of oppression.

What interests me is the reasoning underneath the convention: how preferences develop, how expectations constrain choices, how individual decisions reproduce social patterns, and how we evaluate those patterns without reducing the entire problem to a single principle.

I suppose that’s a lot of analysis for a dinner bill. But the dinner bill was never really the point.
