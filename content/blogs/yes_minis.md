# Can One Be Slightly Moral?

I was recently thinking about Yes, Minister, the British political sitcom, and a line attributed to Sir Humphrey Appleby that morality cannot be a matter of degree. Something is either morally right or morally wrong; one cannot be slightly moral.

It is a brilliant line. It is also, I think, a rather flawed argument.

Not necessarily because categorical moral judgments are impossible, but because I am not sure they can be extended so easily from individual actions to the people who perform them, much less to the institutions through which entire societies act.

Actions, Agents, and the Problem of Aggregation

Suppose we could establish that a particular action was morally wrong. What follows about the person who performed it?

Very little, at least without further information.

An action exists within a context: intentions, circumstances, available alternatives, imperfect knowledge, competing obligations, and consequences that may be impossible to anticipate. Even before judging an action, we must decide what exactly we are judging and according to which principles.

But the problem becomes more difficult when we move from actions to agents.

A person is not a single action. A person is a collection of actions, decisions, omissions, intentions, and changing dispositions across time. Some of these may be generous, others selfish; some courageous, others cowardly. People contradict themselves. They learn, regress, reconsider, and occasionally surprise themselves.

How, then, do we aggregate these observations into a categorical judgment about a person?

Counting actions seems arbitrary. Weighting them by their consequences introduces further judgments. Considering intentions complicates matters further still. And whatever assessment we arrive at describes a person who continues to change.

Perhaps we can judge individual actions categorically under a particular moral framework. But it does not follow that the person performing them must be either categorically moral or categorically immoral.

Why should a process composed of many heterogeneous actions inherit a binary classification from the individual actions themselves?

What About the State?

The problem becomes more pronounced when we consider political institutions.

A democratic state is not a person with a unified mind. It is a collection of individuals with different values, interests, and conceptions of the good, acting through institutions, elections, negotiations, laws, and compromises.

A government might protect employment while enabling harm abroad. It might preserve stability while perpetuating injustice. It might advance one group’s interests at another’s expense.

To call such a government simply moral or immoral risks concealing the very conflicts that make its decisions difficult.

And yet governments must act. They cannot suspend every decision until moral philosophy has settled its disagreements. They must choose among alternatives, evaluate consequences, and accept trade-offs.

This is what makes Humphrey’s argument interesting. His categorical language coexists with practical reasoning about stability, economic interests, and political continuity. The rhetoric is binary, but the decisions involve competing considerations.

Of course, one could consistently believe that actions possess objective moral properties while acknowledging that policymakers cannot always know what those properties are. Uncertainty about the right action does not, by itself, disprove the existence of a right answer.

But that is precisely the distinction worth making: the possibility of categorical moral truth is different from our ability to identify it, and both are different from our ability to embody it consistently.

# The Absurdity of Moral Certainty

I approach this problem from an absurdist disposition.

I am not convinced that the universe supplies an intrinsic moral order, or that human beings can appeal to some final authority to settle every disagreement about how we ought to live. We find ourselves existing, conscious of ourselves and others, making decisions without any guarantee that our principles are ultimately correct.

This does not necessarily mean that all actions are equivalent, or that suffering and well-being are irrelevant. It means that our reasons for caring about them must be examined rather than treated as self-evident truths handed down by the universe.

We construct moral frameworks, inherit others, revise them, and argue about them. We discover inconsistencies in our behavior and in the principles we profess. Sometimes we recognize that we were wrong; sometimes we merely become less certain that we were right.

I find it difficult to reconcile this condition with the idea that moral character must be a categorical property.

Perhaps we can make categorical judgments within a framework. But our frameworks themselves remain open to examination, and our capacity to act according to them is limited by ignorance, conflicting motivations, and circumstances beyond our control.

There is no obvious reason to expect imperfect agents operating under these conditions to produce morally perfect lives.

Can We Be Slightly More Moral Than Yesterday?

This brings me to a more modest proposition.

Perhaps the most we can reasonably aspire to is to act slightly more morally than we did yesterday.

I do not mean that morality can be reduced to a numerical score, or that every day provides a clear opportunity for improvement. Nor do I mean that good intentions excuse serious harm. Some actions demand accountability regardless of the agent’s broader character.

I mean something simpler: we can examine our previous decisions, recognize the harm we caused, reconsider the assumptions behind our conduct, and attempt to do better.

We can reduce suffering without knowing whether suffering is the ultimate foundation of morality. We can treat other people with greater consideration without claiming to have solved the problem of moral obligation. We can change our minds without pretending that our latest position is final.

Progress, in this sense, does not require moral certainty. It requires the willingness to remain corrigible.

There is a tension here. To call tomorrow’s behavior more moral than today’s, I must already possess some standard by which improvement can be recognized. I cannot escape making normative commitments simply by rejecting an objective moral order.

Perhaps that is unavoidable. Even the decision to reduce harm rather than increase it reflects a commitment, not a conclusion forced upon us by the structure of the universe.

But I do not think this makes the effort meaningless. A commitment does not need to be cosmically guaranteed before it can shape how we live.

# An Unfinished Conclusion

I am not proposing a new moral theory, and I am not certain that the argument is internally complete.

I am questioning the movement from the categorical evaluation of actions to the categorical evaluation of agents. I am questioning whether a democratic collective can meaningfully be assigned a single moral character. And I am questioning whether the absence of a final moral foundation leaves us with anything other than indifference.

My tentative answer to the last question is no. Uncertainty does not compel indifference.

We can continue to care, to reason, to disagree, and to revise our behavior without pretending that we possess a final answer. We can acknowledge that our moral judgments are fallible while still taking responsibility for the consequences of our choices.

Perhaps we cannot establish that we are moral people. Perhaps the category itself is less useful than we assume.

But we can still ask whether we have done better, whether we have caused less harm, whether we have treated others more fairly, and whether we are willing to correct ourselves when we fail.

I find that a more defensible aspiration than the claim that morality must be categorical.

Not moral perfection. Not moral certainty.

Just the possibility of being slightly more moral than yesterday, even if we can never be entirely sure what that means.
