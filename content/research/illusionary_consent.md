# The Illusion of Boundaries: Determinism, Consent, and the Conditions of Reason

The world evolves. Whatever we are, whatever we think, whatever we choose to do, we are part of this evolution. There is no free will in the sense of an uncaused origin of action. The state of the world produces the next state of the world, and we are among the things through which this transition occurs.

We can still understand cause and effect. We can construct abstract principles, develop social contracts, formulate universal maxims, and reason about the kinds of worlds we want to inhabit. None of this requires us to stand outside causality. Our understanding of the world is itself a part of the world, and whatever we understand can become a cause of what happens next.

But there is a peculiar problem when we begin asking questions about the individual.

Where does the individual begin and end? Even in reinforcement learning, a mathematically formalized world of states, agents, and decision processes, the problem of defining the boundaries of the agent and its environment remains conceptually nontrivial, with no universally settled formulation. We conventionally draw a boundary around an organism and call it a self. Yet the things that constitute this self—its preferences, beliefs, memories, dispositions, and even its capacity to reason—are products of causes that extend far beyond it. The past is not merely something that happened to us. It continues to operate through us.

Reinforcement learning offers a useful formal analogue to this problem. Nan Jiang’s [*On Value Functions and the Agent-Environment Boundary*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.13341) examines how the placement of that boundary affects the mathematical formulation of value functions. Meanwhile, [*Reinforcement Learning: A Brief Guide for Philosophers of Mind*](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/phc3.12865) explores the framework’s implications for questions about cognition and agency, while [*Reinforcement Learning and Artificial Agency*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/mila.12458) examines what it means for a system to qualify as an agent in the first place. Even in this abstract, mathematically formalized setting, the definition of an agent is not entirely independent of how we model the system. The analogy is not that reinforcement learning has settled the philosophical problem of the self, but that even when we formalize the world in terms of agents and environments, the boundary between them remains a substantive question.

The distinction between what is internal and what is external becomes considerably less obvious when we consider that the internal was produced by the external, and that the external continues to shape the internal. There is no clean separation between the world and the organism that inhabits it. There is only a world state evolving, with what we call the self being one of its configurations.

This becomes particularly troublesome when we consider consent.

Consent appears to require a boundary around an individual whose authorization matters. We ask whether a person agreed, whether the decision was theirs, whether they understood what they were agreeing to, whether they were coerced or manipulated. These are important questions. But what exactly are we identifying when we say that a decision was genuinely theirs?

If every preference, every evaluative disposition, and every mechanism of deliberation has been shaped by prior causes, then there is no ultimate author standing behind the decision. Nor is there an obvious point at which external influence ends and the authentic self begins. Even our ability to distinguish our own reasons from the influence of others is itself a product of causal history.

Consider a person whose preferences have been shaped by years of social conditioning. At what point does that conditioning become manipulation? Is it when the influence is sufficiently strong, when it bypasses deliberation, when it involves deception, or when it produces a preference that would otherwise not have existed? These distinctions may be practically necessary, but they do not appear to follow automatically from the causal structure of the world.

Ordinary education changes us. So does persuasion, culture, friendship, advertising, coercion, and manipulation. These processes differ in important ways, but there is no obvious metaphysical boundary separating external influence from internal preference. The question is not whether a decision has causes. It necessarily does. The question is what kinds of causal processes should count as valid authorization.

I do not think this makes consent meaningless. It does, however, make me suspicious of attempts to ground consent in an independently originating self. Perhaps consent is a practical and normative construct through which we regulate interactions between organisms, rather than some fundamental property waiting to be discovered within them. We can retain the concept without pretending that it rests on metaphysical independence.

But this raises a more interesting question. If the boundaries of the self are so difficult to establish, why should we assume that the boundaries of cognition and reason are any less problematic?

I doubt that reason could have emerged in anything resembling its present form without some medium for reflection. Another person, a drawing on stone, a book, a written proposition, a system of notation: these are not merely convenient instruments for communicating thoughts that have already been fully formed. They allow thought to become an object of thought.

There is a difference between arriving at an intuitive judgment and examining that judgment as a proposition. I can believe something without being able to articulate why I believe it. But once I represent that belief, I can inspect it, compare it against other beliefs, identify contradictions, examine its implications, and revise it. The representation persists beyond the transient cognitive event that produced it. It becomes something upon which further cognition can operate.

This is what makes reflection interesting. Cognition becomes an object available to cognition.

I would distinguish this from the more general information processing that occurs within a nervous system. An organism can learn, predict, discriminate, and respond without necessarily engaging in reflective reasoning. The existence of sophisticated inference-like processes does not, by itself, establish the existence of reason in the stronger sense I have in mind: the capacity to make judgments objects of examination and to assess them according to rules.

The philosophical tradition, Kant in particular, offers resources for thinking about the distinction between sensibility, understanding, and judgment. I am not committed to Kant's entire system, nor do I think the question can be settled simply by appealing to him. What interests me is the distinction between having representations and being able to examine how those representations function in judgment.

Could such reflection arise entirely within an isolated individual, without language, external artifacts, or interaction with other minds? I am skeptical. At the very least, I suspect we substantially underestimate the extent to which our capacity for reason depends on representational structures that are not reducible to an isolated organism.

Language itself is inherited. The concepts with which we examine our beliefs are socially developed. The notation with which we express logical relations is historically accumulated. Books preserve reasoning that can be inspected long after the people who produced it are gone. Other minds expose contradictions that our own cognitive processes fail to detect.

When I reason, then, how much of that reasoning is exclusively mine?

The question is not merely whether other people have influenced my thoughts. Of course they have. It is whether the very capacity through which I identify, evaluate, and revise those thoughts is partly constituted by processes that extend beyond the individual. If so, the individual is not simply a bounded container in which reasoning occurs. Reason may be a process distributed across organisms, language, artifacts, and accumulated knowledge.

The boundary between the individual and the world begins to look less like a fundamental division and more like a distinction useful for certain purposes. The organism remains real. Its physical boundaries matter. But those boundaries need not coincide with the boundaries of cognition, agency, or reflective reason.

And perhaps this brings us back to consent.

If the preferences by which I consent are causally produced by the world, and the rational faculties by which I evaluate those preferences are themselves partly constituted through interactions with the world, then what exactly justifies treating the individual as an independently originating authority over the decision?

I do not have a complete answer. Nor do I think the absence of such an answer licenses us to disregard consent. The practical importance of consent may survive the failure of its conventional metaphysical foundations. We can still distinguish coercion from persuasion, deception from informed agreement, and the violation of bodily boundaries from respect for them. These distinctions can matter enormously without requiring an uncaused self.

Perhaps the mistake is to expect ethics to rest on a metaphysically independent agent in the first place. We are configurations of a causally evolving world, capable of representing possible states of that world, understanding some of their consequences, and constructing principles to constrain what happens next.

The principles we formulate become part of the causal process. They change what we do, what we build, what we permit, and what we prevent. There is no contradiction in a deterministic world containing systems that understand determinism and act according to abstract principles derived from that understanding.

What remains unresolved is how to determine which principles ought to govern those transitions, and why certain configurations—suffering, coercion, the destruction of agency, the disregard of another organism's preferences—should count as reasons against a particular course of action. But that is a different problem from establishing an uncaused author of action.

The world evolves. We are part of that evolution. We can understand some of its causal structure, construct principles, and make those principles causally consequential.

***Perhaps the more productive question is not where the self ends, where reason begins, or where consent originates in some ultimate sense. It is what distinctions we can justify within a world in which none of these things exists independently of the causal processes that produce and sustain them.***
